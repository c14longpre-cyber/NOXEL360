What Is Disaster Recovery? A Simple Explanation

In one sentence: Disaster recovery is a documented plan for restoring systems and operations after a major disruption — a plan that goes beyond just having backups to include exactly how, in what order, and by whom things get restored.

Backups answer "do we have a copy of our data?" Disaster recovery answers a broader, more practical question: "if something serious happens, what exactly do we do, in what order, to get back up and running?"

What a Real Disaster Recovery Plan Includes

  • What counts as a disaster — hardware failure, cyberattack, natural disaster, human error
  • Priority order — which systems need to come back online first
  • Clear roles — who is responsible for which part of recovery
  • Step-by-step recovery procedures — the actual technical steps to restore each system
  • Communication plan — how customers, staff, and stakeholders are kept informed during the incident

Two Important Planning Metrics

RTO (Recovery Time Objective) — how quickly a system needs to be operational again after a disruption. A critical customer-facing system might need an RTO of minutes; an internal reporting tool might tolerate a full day.

RPO (Recovery Point Objective) — how much recent data loss is acceptable. An RPO of one hour means, at most, an hour's worth of the most recent data could be lost in a worst-case scenario.

These two numbers directly shape how aggressive (and expensive) a recovery plan needs to be — near-zero RTO and RPO require significantly more investment than more relaxed targets.

Why Planning in Advance Matters So Much

During an actual incident, stress and time pressure make it easy to miss steps or waste time figuring things out on the fly. A documented plan, created calmly in advance, removes much of that uncertainty exactly when clear thinking is hardest to come by.

A Practical Starting Point for Small Businesses

A formal enterprise disaster recovery plan isn't necessary for every business — but even a simple, written document covering "what are our critical systems, where are our backups, who do we contact, and in what order do we restore things" provides real, meaningful protection over having no plan at all.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is disaster recovery the same as having backups?

Backups are one component of disaster recovery, not the whole plan. Disaster recovery also includes the process, roles, and priorities for actually restoring operations after a major disruption.

Do small businesses need a formal disaster recovery plan?

Even a simple, documented plan is far better than none — knowing in advance what to do, who to contact, and what to restore first can meaningfully reduce downtime during an actual incident.

What are RTO and RPO?

Recovery Time Objective (RTO) is how quickly a system needs to be back online. Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is how much recent data loss is acceptable. Both help define how aggressive a recovery plan needs to be.
