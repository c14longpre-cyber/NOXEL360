What Is Disaster Recovery? A Simple Explanation

In one sentence: Disaster recovery is a documented plan for restoring systems and operations after a major disruption — a plan that goes beyond just having backups to include exactly how, in what order, and by whom things get restored.

Backups answer "do we have a copy of our data?" Disaster recovery answers a broader question: "if something serious happens, what exactly do we do, in what order, to get back up and running?"

What a Real Disaster Recovery Plan Includes

  • What counts as a disaster — hardware failure, cyberattack, natural disaster, human error
  • Priority order — which systems come back online first
  • Clear roles — who is responsible for what
  • Step-by-step procedures — the actual technical restoration steps
  • Communication plan — keeping customers and staff informed
💡 Did You Know?

Many disaster recovery plans fail not because they don't exist, but because they were never actually tested — the first real test happening during an actual crisis is a common, costly mistake.

Two Important Planning Metrics

RTO (Recovery Time Objective) — how quickly a system needs to be operational again. RPO (Recovery Point Objective) — how much recent data loss is acceptable. Both shape how aggressive a plan needs to be.

Why Planning in Advance Matters So Much

During an actual incident, stress and time pressure make it easy to miss steps. A documented plan, created calmly in advance, removes much of that uncertainty.

A Practical Starting Point for Small Businesses

A formal enterprise plan isn't necessary for every business, but even a simple written document covering critical systems, backups, and contacts provides real protection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is disaster recovery the same as having backups?

Backups are one component of disaster recovery, not the whole plan. Disaster recovery also includes process, roles, and priorities for restoring operations.

Do small businesses need a formal disaster recovery plan?

Even a simple, documented plan is far better than none — knowing what to do and who to contact meaningfully reduces downtime.

What are RTO and RPO?

RTO is how quickly a system needs to be back online. RPO is how much recent data loss is acceptable. Both define how aggressive a recovery plan needs to be.

Who should be involved in creating a disaster recovery plan?

Ideally anyone responsible for critical systems, plus leadership who can make decisions during an actual incident.

How often should a disaster recovery plan be tested?

At least annually, or after any significant infrastructure change, to confirm it still reflects how systems actually work.

What's a common mistake in disaster recovery planning?

Writing a plan but never actually testing it, so gaps or outdated steps only surface during a real crisis.

Can disaster recovery plans cover non-technical disasters too?

Yes — many plans also address scenarios like losing key staff, a facility being unavailable, or major supplier disruptions.

Key Takeaways

  • Disaster recovery goes beyond backups to include process, roles, and priorities.
  • RTO and RPO define how quickly systems must recover and how much data loss is acceptable.
  • A documented plan removes uncertainty exactly when clear thinking is hardest.
  • Even a simple written plan is far better protection than having none.
  • Testing a plan in advance prevents discovering gaps during an actual crisis.
