Common Cyber Threats: A Simple Guide

In one sentence: Most cyberattacks fall into a handful of well-known categories — phishing, malware, ransomware, and credential theft — each exploiting a different weakness, technical or human.

Phishing

Phishing tricks a person into revealing sensitive information — usually login credentials — through a fake email, message, or website designed to look legitimate. It remains one of the most common ways attackers gain initial access, precisely because it targets human trust rather than a technical flaw.

Malware

Malware is any malicious software designed to damage, disrupt, or gain unauthorized access to a system. It covers a broad category, including viruses, spyware, and trojans — each designed for a slightly different malicious purpose.

Ransomware

Ransomware encrypts a victim's files, making them inaccessible, then demands payment for the decryption key. It's one of the most disruptive attack types for businesses, since it can halt operations entirely until resolved — good, tested backups are the strongest defense against needing to face this decision at all.

Credential Theft

Attackers steal usernames and passwords — through phishing, data breaches at other services, or malware — then reuse them to access accounts. This is a major reason unique passwords per account and multi-factor authentication matter so much: reused passwords let a single breach compromise many accounts at once.

Social Engineering

Beyond phishing specifically, social engineering broadly describes manipulating people into breaking normal security practices — impersonating a trusted colleague, creating false urgency, or exploiting helpfulness. It's a reminder that security isn't purely a technical problem; human awareness matters just as much.

Why Layered Defense Matters

No single defense stops every threat category. This is why real security relies on multiple overlapping layers — strong authentication, backups, staff awareness, and monitoring — so a single point of failure doesn't mean total compromise.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common way businesses get hacked?

Phishing remains one of the most common entry points — tricking a person into revealing credentials or clicking a malicious link, rather than exploiting a technical vulnerability directly.

Should I pay a ransomware demand?

Security experts and law enforcement generally advise against paying, since it doesn't guarantee data recovery and funds further attacks. Good backups are the best protection against needing to make that decision at all.

Can cyber threats target individuals, not just businesses?

Yes. Phishing, malware, and credential theft target individuals constantly, not just organizations — personal accounts are common targets too.
