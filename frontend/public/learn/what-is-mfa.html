What Is MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication)?

In one sentence: MFA requires two or more separate forms of proof to log into an account, so a stolen password alone isn't enough for an attacker to get in.

Passwords get stolen constantly — through data breaches, phishing, or malware — often without the account owner even knowing. MFA is the single most effective, widely available defense against a stolen password actually being used to break in.

The Three Categories of "Factors"

  • Something you know — a password or PIN
  • Something you have — a phone, authenticator app, or hardware security key
  • Something you are — a fingerprint or face scan

MFA requires at least two of these different categories together. Even if an attacker steals your password (something you know), they still don't have your phone (something you have) — so they're blocked.

Common Types of MFA in Practice

SMS codes — a one-time code texted to your phone (convenient, but vulnerable to SIM-swapping attacks).

Authenticator apps — apps like Google Authenticator or Authy generate rotating codes, more secure than SMS.

Push notifications — approve a login request directly from an app on your phone.

Hardware security keys — physical devices (like a YubiKey) plugged in or tapped to verify identity, among the most secure options available.

Why MFA Is Considered So High-Impact

Passwords alone are a single point of failure — reused across sites, phished, or leaked in a breach, and suddenly compromised. MFA adds a second, independent barrier that dramatically reduces the practical risk of a stolen password translating into actual unauthorized access, without requiring a complete overhaul of how people log in.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is MFA the same as two-factor authentication (2FA)?

2FA is a specific case of MFA using exactly two factors. MFA is the broader term, covering two or more factors.

Is an authenticator app better than SMS codes?

Generally yes — SMS codes can be intercepted through SIM-swapping attacks, while authenticator apps and hardware keys are considered more secure alternatives.

Does MFA make logging in much slower?

It adds a few extra seconds, and many systems remember a trusted device for a period of time, meaning MFA isn't required on every single login.
