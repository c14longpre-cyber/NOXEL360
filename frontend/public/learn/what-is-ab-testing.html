What Is A/B Testing? A Simple Explanation

In one sentence: A/B testing compares two versions of a page or element — showing each to a portion of real visitors — to measure, with actual data, which one performs better on a specific goal.

Instead of guessing which headline, button color, or layout will perform better, A/B testing lets real visitor behavior settle the question directly, with actual numbers rather than opinions.

How an A/B Test Actually Works

  1. Create two versions of something — "A" (the original) and "B" (the variation)
  2. Show each version to a separate portion of real traffic, randomly split
  3. Track a specific goal (clicks, sign-ups, purchases) for each version
  4. After enough data accumulates, compare results to see which version performed better

What Gets A/B Tested

  • Headlines and copy
  • Call-to-action button text, color, or placement
  • Page layouts
  • Images or visuals
  • Pricing displays
  • Email subject lines

Why "Statistical Significance" Actually Matters

A small difference in results after a handful of visitors could easily be random chance rather than a real effect. Statistical significance is a way of checking whether an observed difference is large and consistent enough to trust as a genuine result, rather than noise — running a test too briefly or with too little traffic risks drawing a confident conclusion from what's actually random variation.

A/B Testing and CRO Work Together

CRO (Conversion Rate Optimization) is the overall goal — improving how well a page converts visitors. A/B testing is one of the primary tools used to actually validate whether a proposed CRO improvement genuinely works, rather than just assuming it does.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much traffic do I need to run a valid A/B test?

It depends on the baseline conversion rate and the size of the difference you're trying to detect, but low-traffic sites often need to run tests for a longer period to reach a statistically reliable result.

Can I test more than two versions at once?

Yes — testing more than two variants is sometimes called multivariate testing, though it typically requires even more traffic to reach reliable conclusions across all the variations.

What happens if a test result isn't statistically significant?

It means there isn't enough confidence yet to declare a real winner — the honest conclusion is to keep testing longer or accept the result as inconclusive, rather than picking a version based on a weak signal.
