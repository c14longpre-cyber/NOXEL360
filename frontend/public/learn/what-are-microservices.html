What Are Microservices? A Simple Explanation

In one sentence: Microservices are an approach to building software as a collection of small, independent services — each responsible for one specific job — instead of one large, tightly connected application.

To understand microservices, it helps to first understand what they're an alternative to: the "monolith."

The Monolith: One Big Application

A monolithic application is built as a single, unified codebase. User accounts, payments, notifications, search — all of it lives in one deployable unit. This is simpler to build and reason about early on, but as the application grows, everything becomes more tightly coupled: a small change in one area risks breaking something unrelated, and the entire application has to be redeployed for any update.

Microservices: Many Small, Independent Services

With microservices, that same application is broken into separate, independently deployable services — a "payments service," a "notifications service," a "search service" — each with its own codebase, often its own database, communicating with the others through APIs.

Each service can be updated, scaled, or even rewritten independently, without touching the others. If the notifications service needs to handle 10x more traffic, you can scale just that piece — not the entire application.

The Real Trade-Off

Microservices solve real problems at scale, but they introduce real complexity too: more moving parts to monitor, more network calls between services (each one a potential point of failure), and more coordination required across teams. This is why microservices tend to make sense for larger, more mature products — and often actively slow down small teams or early-stage products that don't yet have the scale to justify the overhead.

A Practical Rule of Thumb

Start with a well-organized monolith. Split out a microservice only when you have a clear, specific reason — a part of the system that needs to scale independently, or a team that needs to move independently from the rest. Splitting too early adds complexity without a matching benefit.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are microservices always better than a monolith?

No. Microservices add real operational complexity. For small products or early-stage startups, a well-organized monolith is often faster to build and easier to maintain.

Do I need microservices to scale my product?

Not necessarily. Many products scale to significant size on a monolith before microservices become genuinely necessary. Scaling problems are often about specific bottlenecks, not the overall architecture.

How do microservices communicate with each other?

Usually through APIs — typically REST or messaging queues — the same core concept covered in "What Is an API," just applied between internal services instead of external ones.
