What Is SSL/TLS? A Simple Explanation

In one sentence: SSL and TLS are the cryptographic protocols that actually do the encryption behind HTTPS — they're the reason your padlock icon appears and your data stays private in transit.

If HTTPS is the outcome you see (a secure connection, a padlock), SSL/TLS is the actual mechanism making it happen underneath. Understanding the relationship: HTTPS = HTTP + SSL/TLS encryption.

SSL vs TLS: Why Two Names for Similar Things

SSL (Secure Sockets Layer) was the original protocol, developed in the 1990s. It had known security weaknesses and was eventually replaced by TLS (Transport Layer Security) — a more secure successor. Technically, every modern "SSL certificate" today is actually using TLS encryption; the term "SSL" just stuck around as the common industry name, even though the underlying technology has moved on.

What a Certificate Actually Does

An SSL/TLS certificate does two things at once:

  • Encrypts data traveling between the visitor's browser and the server, so it can't be read if intercepted
  • Verifies identity — confirming the website is actually who it claims to be, issued by a trusted Certificate Authority

Without that identity verification piece, encryption alone wouldn't be enough — you could have a perfectly encrypted connection to an impostor site. The certificate solves both problems together.

How a Certificate Gets Issued

A website owner requests a certificate from a Certificate Authority (CA), who verifies they genuinely control the domain, then issues a certificate the browser can trust. Free, automated options like Let's Encrypt made this process essentially frictionless — most modern hosting platforms request and renew certificates automatically with zero manual work required.

Certificates Expire — and That Matters

SSL/TLS certificates aren't permanent; they expire (often every 90 days for free options) and must be renewed. If a certificate lapses, visitors' browsers will show alarming security warnings, sometimes blocking access to the site entirely — a real, visible failure that most modern hosting automates away, but is worth knowing about if managing infrastructure manually.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is SSL still used, or has it been fully replaced by TLS?

SSL has been technically obsolete for years and modern systems use TLS exclusively — but "SSL" stuck around as the common industry term people still use to refer to what is, technically, TLS encryption.

Do I need to renew an SSL/TLS certificate?

Yes, certificates expire (often every 90 days for free options like Let's Encrypt). Most modern hosting platforms handle renewal automatically, but self-managed setups need this tracked manually to avoid a lapsed certificate.

What happens if a certificate expires?

Visitors' browsers will show a security warning and may block access to the site entirely until the certificate is renewed — a serious, visible problem that erodes trust immediately.
